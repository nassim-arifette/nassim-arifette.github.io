## The problem

On the way to Mars, a doctor on Earth answers up to twenty minutes late. The crew needs to see on board what their body is doing, what needs attention today, and what NASA would check, without waiting for the ground.

We built Pulsar for the NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026, for the challenge *Create Health Monitoring Software for Astronauts on Space Missions*.

## What it does

- **The body, in 3D.** Every part Pulsar follows has a marker on the body: white when it's fine, amber or red when it isn't. Click one and a card opens with the value, its 30-day trend, what to do, and the exam NASA runs in flight for the same question. A depth control goes from the spacesuit down to the organs and the skeleton.
- **Today's check-up.** A planner turns the signals into the day's tasks: exams due, NASA's tasks for that flight day, self-tests and follow-ups. Every task traces back to a value.
- **Self-tests with the webcam.** Thirteen short tests modelled on NASA practice (reaction time, a cognition battery, vision, hearing, balance, dexterity, pulse recovery). The camera counts repetitions and reads pulse and breathing from the skin and chest. Results stay on the computer.
- **A day on the mission.** A simulation plays a full day at each destination: a CO₂ scrubber failure on the station, a solar storm on the way to Mars. The dose turns red and the check-up rewrites itself.
- **Through the helmet visor.** An AR view puts the vital signs on the rim of a spacewalk visor, and can be driven with the eyes through webcam gaze tracking.
- **The report to Earth.** The day's report is encrypted and sent over a link with the destination's delay, from one second in orbit to twenty minutes from Mars.

## Team and scope

Built by Lucas Baurianne, Enzo Baurianne, Nassim Arifette and Youssef Mir. Pulsar runs entirely in the browser with no server. It is not affiliated with NASA and is not a medical device: the crew records are made up for the demo.
